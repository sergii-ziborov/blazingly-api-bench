# Five-implementation comparison — 2026-07-28

Raw numbers: [`20260728-171644-summary.txt`](20260728-171644-summary.txt).

Configuration: 64 connections, 8s per sample, 2s warmup, **4 rounds**, 4 workers,
interleaved. Five implementations: blazingly (3201), axum (3202), actix (3203),
tokio (3204, bare hyper, no framework), fastapi (3205).

## Read this first: the host was not idle

| | avg | min | max |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Background CPU before the run** | **85.2%** | 80.5% | 87.7% |
| **Background CPU after the run** | **62.7%** | 19.9% | 86.0% |

Measured with `Get-Counter "\Processor(_Total)\% Processor Time"`, 10 one-second
samples at each end, on a 14-logical-processor machine.

Two things follow, and they govern everything below.

**There was roughly two cores of headroom for a benchmark that saturates four
workers.** The servers were not competing against an idle machine, they were
competing against Chrome, an esbuild watcher, Telegram and two other agent
processes for the same cores.

**The load moved by 22 percentage points between the start and the end of the
run**, and per-sample readings ranged from 19.9% to 86%. The environment at the
end of the matrix was not the environment at the start, so even a perfectly
interleaved design cannot fully subtract it.

The consequence is measurable rather than hypothetical. Repeating one
implementation on one scenario four times produced spreads of **1.27x to 4.18x**.
That is the noise band, and it is wider than almost every difference between
implementations.

On the four read scenarios the implementations are closer to each other than
each one is to itself. There is no ranking to report.

The sharpest illustration: **on two of five scenarios, the choice of median
convention changes which implementation "wins."**

- `list` — leader is actix by true median (14,204), blazingly by upper median (16,239)
- `search` — leader is blazingly by true median (15,875), tokio by upper median (17,902)

Nothing about the frameworks changed between those two columns. Only the
arithmetic did. Any headline drawn from the read scenarios is an artifact of
which sample the median happened to land on.

## What the data supports

**1. All four Rust implementations beat FastAPI on every read scenario, by
roughly 5x to 7x.** Supported: the sample ranges do not overlap at all. The
slowest Rust sample on `detail` (7,952) is still 2.5x the fastest FastAPI sample
(3,160). The same holds on `list`, `filter` and `search`. This gap is far larger
than the noise band and survives any median convention.

**2. Memory is the most robust number in the entire run.** Rust implementations
sat at 24.5–30.5 MiB peak RSS, FastAPI at 296–306 MiB, in every single sample.
That is a ~11x difference with no overlap and essentially no variance, because
resident memory does not care what else the host is doing. `tokio` was the
lowest at a flat 24.5 MiB.

**3. On bulk ingestion, `blazingly` is genuinely and badly slower — and this one
is not noise.** blazingly's *best* bulk sample (1,142 rps) is below the *worst*
sample of axum (3,299), actix (3,914) and tokio (6,534). Non-overlapping ranges,
10.17x on the medians, against a noise band that tops out at 4.18x.

The CPU column says why it is worth investigating rather than dismissing:
blazingly used **117%** CPU on bulk (33%–137% across rounds) while tokio used
283% and axum 258% on the identical workload. Every implementation had four
workers and 64 concurrent connections. blazingly is not using the cores
available to it — the signature of a serialization point in the ingest path, not
of slow validation code. On the read scenarios blazingly reaches 250–330% CPU
normally, so whatever this is, it is specific to bulk ingest.

**4. On bulk, `blazingly` and FastAPI are indistinguishable.** blazingly
273–1,142, FastAPI 636–1,252; the samples interleave completely. A Rust
framework landing on the same line as CPython on the one write-heavy scenario is
the single most actionable result here.

**5. Bare `tokio` beats `actix` on bulk** (6,534 min vs 4,956 max, no overlap).
Against `axum` it is only marginal — tokio's range 6,534–9,138 overlaps axum's
3,299–7,140 — so "tokio is fastest at ingestion" is directionally right but not
cleanly separated from axum.

## What the data does NOT support

**Any ranking among blazingly, axum, actix and tokio on `list`, `detail`,
`filter` or `search`.** The between-implementation gaps (1.13x–1.44x) are
smaller than the within-implementation spreads (up to 3.99x), and the leader
flips with the median convention on two of the four. On this host, on this run,
those four implementations are one undifferentiated group on read workloads.

Specifically unsupported, though the summary table's ordering might suggest
otherwise:

- that blazingly is faster than axum on reads
- that the framework-free tokio baseline is faster than the frameworks on reads
  (it is mid-pack on `list` and `detail`, and its 3.99x spread on `detail` is the
  worst in the run)
- that any of the read-scenario p50 differences of a millisecond or two mean
  anything

## FastAPI's sample count, and why its numbers flatter it

Seven of FastAPI's twenty samples were discarded (`n=2` or `n=3` rather than 4):

```
fastapi list   round 2   13 connection errors
fastapi bulk   round 2   16 connection errors
fastapi list   round 3   17 connection errors
fastapi search round 3   11 connection errors
fastapi detail round 4   14 connection errors
fastapi filter round 4   17 connection errors
fastapi search round 4   20 connection errors
```

All seven were **connection errors, zero status mismatches**. uvicorn dropped
keep-alive connections under sustained load; it never answered a request
differently. The contract equivalence is intact — that was independently
confirmed by the gate (120/120 checks).

But the bias runs one way and should be stated: the discarded samples are the
ones where FastAPI was under the most stress, so **FastAPI's medians here are
optimistic**, not pessimistic. Its true sustained numbers on this host are at or
below what the tables show. No other implementation lost a single sample; all 80
Rust samples completed with zero errors and zero mismatches.

## Equivalence

The gate passed before measurement: **120/120 checks agree across blazingly,
axum, actix, tokio, fastapi** (24 cases x 5 implementations). No throughput in
this document comes from a server that answered differently from the others.

## How much of this is worth anything

The memory comparison and the bulk-ingest finding are solid. They rest on
non-overlapping ranges with large margins, and the bulk finding is corroborated
by an independent signal (the CPU column) rather than by throughput alone.

The Rust-versus-FastAPI read gap is solid in direction and rough magnitude,
though "5x to 7x" should not be sharpened past that.

Everything else — every comparison among the four Rust implementations on read
workloads — should be treated as unmeasured. Not "close", not "roughly tied
with blazingly slightly ahead": **unmeasured**. Re-running this on a quiet
machine is the only way to turn it into data, and until then the read-scenario
tables are a record of what the host was doing, not of what the frameworks do.
