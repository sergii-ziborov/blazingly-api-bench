# Five-implementation comparison — 2026-07-29

Re-run of the full matrix after two framework fixes landed in `blazingly`
(`4438df5`, "specialise pattern matching, and keep uploads out of the JSON
document"). It supersedes [`20260728-171644-analysis.md`](20260728-171644-analysis.md),
which was taken on a host at 85% background load and reported the four Rust
implementations as one undifferentiated group.

Configuration: 64 connections, 8s per sample, 2s warmup, **5 rounds**, 4 workers,
interleaved. blazingly (3201), axum (3202), actix (3203), tokio (3204, bare
hyper, no framework), fastapi (3205).

## Equivalence, before anything was measured

**120/120 checks agree across blazingly, axum, actix, tokio, fastapi**
(24 cases x 5 implementations). No throughput below comes from a server that
answered differently from the others.

The five also agree on the upload endpoint, which the gate does not cover: all
five returned byte-identical `{"id":1,...,"bytes":5242880,"content_type":"image/jpeg"}`
for the 5 MiB multipart body. That was checked by hand before the upload
scenario was measured, not asserted.

## The host was much quieter than last time

| | avg | min | max |
|---|---|---|---|
| Background CPU before the run | **31.3%** | 25.7% | 37.9% |
| Background CPU after the run | **27.8%** | 18.8% | 38.5% |

`Get-Counter "\Processor(_Total)\% Processor Time"`, 10 one-second samples at
each end, 14 logical processors. The previous run was 85.2% before / 62.7%
after, and moved 22 points during the matrix; this one moved 3.5.

That shows up directly in the noise. Single-implementation spreads were
**1.33x–2.04x** here against **1.27x–4.18x** before. The measurement is better,
and more of it survives. It is still not an idle machine.

## The noise band, and what clears it

For each scenario: the gap between the four Rust implementations' medians,
against the widest spread any one of them showed across its own five rounds.

| scenario | gap between the 4 Rust impls | noise band | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| list | 1.39x | 1.40x – 1.66x | **inside the noise** |
| detail | 1.21x | 1.33x – 1.56x | **inside the noise** |
| filter | 1.43x | 1.55x – 1.73x | **inside the noise** |
| search | 1.12x | 1.54x – 2.04x | **inside the noise** |
| **bulk** | **1.70x** | 1.43x – 1.69x | **real** (tokio over actix, ranges do not overlap) |
| **upload** | **1.90x** | 1.01x – 1.09x | **real** (actix over blazingly, ranges do not overlap) |

Two tests are applied throughout, and they are not equally strong. The
noise-band test compares the median gap to the widest single-implementation
spread. The **overlap test** — does the leader's worst sample still beat the
trailer's best? — does not depend on a median convention, and nothing is called
real below unless it passes that one.

With five rounds the true median is a single sample rather than the mean of two,
so the median-convention flip that decided two scenarios in the previous report
cannot occur here. The read scenarios are still inside the noise anyway.

## The pattern-matching fix landed, and it is the largest change in the run

`blazingly` on bulk went from a median of **782 rps to 10,562 rps** between the
two runs. That is the headline, but the two runs were taken on different hosts
in effect (85% vs 31% background load), so the raw 13.5x cannot be attributed to
the fix without an argument. Here is the argument.

The host quieting lifted every scenario. Comparing blazingly against itself
across the two runs:

| scenario | 2026-07-28 median | 2026-07-29 median | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| list | 13,721 | 27,089 | 1.97x |
| detail | 22,138 | 26,006 | 1.17x |
| filter | 13,979 | 22,138 | 1.58x |
| search | 15,875 | 27,171 | 1.71x |
| **bulk** | **782** | **10,562** | **13.51x** |

The read scenarios gained 1.17x–1.97x from the quieter host. Bulk gained 13.5x —
roughly **8x more than the host change explains**. A confounded comparison
cannot be made exact, but it does not need to be: the residual is an order of
magnitude.

The CPU column corroborates it independently, and CPU utilisation is not a
throughput number that host load inflates. blazingly used **117%** CPU on bulk
before and **356%** now, on identical work with the same four workers. The
previous report called 117% "the signature of a serialization point in the
ingest path". That signature is gone.

On bulk, blazingly now sits **second of four**, ahead of axum and actix, behind
bare tokio. And it has separated from FastAPI: its worst sample (8,104) is 4.1x
FastAPI's best (1,977), where previously the two interleaved completely.

## The upload scenario — measured here for the first time

`SPEC.md` has always listed `upload` as a scenario; `run.ps1` never implemented
it. It does now. The body is a committed 5 MiB `multipart/form-data` document
(`tools/generate_cover_payload.py`), byte-identical for all five.

Measured at two concurrencies, because peak RSS on a 5 MiB body is mostly a
question of how many bodies are resident at once.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| axum | 652 | 630 – 662 | 1.05x | 9.28ms | 33.5 MiB | +8.9 | 5 |

Idle baseline, measured separately (server started, one `/health`, no load):
blazingly 25.0 MiB, axum 24.6, actix 24.5, tokio 24.5, fastapi 293.0.

These are the tightest samples in the entire suite — spreads of 1.01x to 1.16x,
because a 5 MiB upload is bounded by copying rather than by scheduling, and
copying is what a loaded host perturbs least.

**blazingly holds roughly two full copies of every in-flight upload; the other
three hold a fraction of one.** Memory above idle, as a multiple of the bytes
actually in flight:

| impl | 8 conn | 32 conn |
|---|---|---|
| blazingly | 2.04x | 1.72x |
| axum | 0.22x | 0.26x |
| actix | 0.19x | 0.14x |
| tokio | 0.29x | 0.32x |

blazingly's overhead grew 3.4x when concurrency grew 4x: it scales per in-flight
request, which is what buffering the whole part does. The other three stay flat
at a fraction of the in-flight bytes, which is what streaming does.

That is a framework capability difference, not an implementation choice. axum's
handler counts and drops the field a chunk at a time (`field.chunk().await` in a
loop); actix and tokio do the same. blazingly's handler uses `File<UploadFile>`,
and its own source comment says why: *"There is no streaming multipart extractor
to reach for instead."*

**What cannot be said: whether the multipart fix improved this.** The harness had
no upload scenario before, so there is no pre-fix upload measurement to compare
against, on this host or any other. The fix may well have removed a much larger
cost — keeping a 5 MiB body out of a JSON document plausibly saved several more
copies — and this could be a large improvement over a worse starting point. The
data here cannot distinguish that from no change. It establishes only where
blazingly stands now, which is last of the four Rust implementations on both
throughput and memory for uploads.

## What the data supports

**1. All four Rust implementations beat FastAPI on every scenario, by 8x–11x on
reads and 6.7x on bulk.** Ranges do not overlap anywhere, by wide margins. On
`list` the gap is 11.35x. This survives any convention and is far outside the
noise band.

**2. Memory is again the most robust number.** The four Rust implementations sit
at 24.5–28.5 MiB across every read and bulk sample; FastAPI at 296.8–303.4 MiB.
~11–12x, with essentially no variance, because resident memory does not care
what else the host is doing. On uploads FastAPI is still highest in absolute
terms (315–345 MiB) but its *overhead* per in-flight byte is lower than
blazingly's.

**3. `blazingly` is no longer slow on bulk ingestion, and is no longer level with
FastAPI there.** Median 782 → 10,562; CPU 117% → 356%; worst sample now 4.1x
FastAPI's best. The single most actionable finding of the previous report has
been resolved.

**4. Bare `tokio` is the fastest on bulk.** 11,963 median, and its worst sample
(8,495) beats actix's best (8,328) — the only clean separation inside the Rust
group on bulk. Against blazingly (8,104–11,588) and axum (5,954–9,668) the
ranges overlap, so tokio's lead over *those two* is directional only.

**5. `blazingly` is materially behind on uploads.** 1.85x–1.90x lower throughput
and 3.0x–6.3x higher peak RSS than axum/actix/tokio, with non-overlapping ranges
at both concurrencies and the tightest spreads in the suite. This is the clearest
single-implementation result in the run.

## What the data does not support

**Any ranking among blazingly, axum, actix and tokio on `list`, `detail`,
`filter` or `search`.** The gaps (1.12x–1.43x) are smaller than the
within-implementation spreads (1.33x–2.04x), and no implementation's worst
sample beats any other's best on any read scenario. Four implementations, one
undifferentiated group — **unmeasured**, not "close".

This needs saying explicitly because the tables are suggestive:
**`blazingly` has the highest median on three of the four read scenarios**
(`list`, `filter`, `search`) and is second on `detail`. That is not nothing, and
it is a change from the previous run. It is also **not a result**. Every one of
those gaps sits inside the noise band, every range overlaps, and a leader
emerging on 3 of 4 scenarios is unremarkable when five implementations are
compared on four scenarios at this spread. Re-running on an idle machine is what
would settle it.

Also unsupported:

- that blazingly beats axum or actix on bulk (10,562 vs 9,021 and 7,050 — the
  gaps are 1.17x and 1.50x against a 1.69x noise band, and the ranges overlap)
- that actix's low bulk CPU (209%, against 325–356% for the others) means
  anything yet; it is a single unexplained column, not a corroborated finding
- that any read-scenario p50 difference of a few tenths of a millisecond means
  anything

## Discarded samples

Three of 155 samples were discarded, all FastAPI, all **connection errors with
zero status mismatches** — uvicorn dropping keep-alive connections under
sustained load, never answering a request differently.

```
fastapi search round 4    8 connection errors   (main matrix)
fastapi search round 5   16 connection errors   (main matrix)
fastapi upload round 5    4 connection errors   (upload, 8 conn)
fastapi upload round 3   11 connection errors   (upload, 32 conn)
```

The bias runs one way: the discarded samples are the ones where FastAPI was under
the most stress, so **its medians here are optimistic**. All 100 Rust samples in
the main matrix completed clean, as did all Rust upload samples.

This is a large improvement over the previous run, where 7 of 20 FastAPI samples
were lost; the quieter host is the likely reason.

## One peak-RSS reading was thrown out, and the collector was fixed

`blazingly/filter` round 5 reported **897.3 MiB** peak RSS. The other four
samples of that cell read 26.4–26.8 MiB, and that sample's throughput (22,867)
and CPU (351%) were unremarkable. Nothing else in 100 Rust samples exceeded
30 MiB.

The cause is the harness, not the server. `Get-ProcessTreeUsage` collected
children by `ParentProcessId` alone; Windows leaves that field pointing at a dead
parent and recycles PIDs aggressively, and a full matrix starts and stops 155
servers. An unrelated process that inherited a recycled PID had its
`PeakWorkingSet64` summed into the reading.

Two changes followed: `run.ps1` now rejects a "child" that predates its parent,
and `tools/analyze_run.py` reports the **median** of the per-round RSS readings
and flags any cell whose max exceeds 4x its median rather than letting one
reading become the headline. The upload runs used the fixed collector; the main
matrix did not, so its memory column is medians over five readings with the
outlier visible and excluded.

## Bottom line

The bulk-ingest regression is fixed and the fix is well supported. Uploads are
now blazingly's clear weak point, also well supported, though the run cannot say
whether the multipart fix improved it or merely left it here. Read throughput
remains unmeasured on this host: blazingly looks good in three of four medians,
and that is a reason to run it on an idle machine, not a reason to publish a
ranking.
